Junkstronaut
Game Design Document — Final  |  Chad Walker — Multi-Agent AI for Game Development, Assignment 
1. Executive Summary
A Kessler cascade has shattered every satellite in orbit into valuable junk. Mr. Armstrong, owner of the junkyard, wants it all brought back down. One piece he wants above the rest: an Apollo-era service module at the very top of the flight envelope. Armstrong flew that module's program as a young man. This Apollo-era module — the one piece that predates and outlived the collapse — is the thing he actually wants back. It's worth more than its mass because it's worth more to him. You fly a rocket built from scrap: launch, get out, tether debris by hand, haul it home, and sell it to Armstrong for upgrades. 2D pixel art, keyboard and mouse only, built in Godot.
Core loop: repair/refuel and upgrade → fly up, either on a ballistic arc or by circularising into orbit  → EVA and collect junk → reenter and land → sell.
Win: land Armstrong's module in pristine (his triumph) or damaged (his disappointment) condition — reachable and landable only near full upgrades (its mass, states, and delivery terms: §2.6), and not landable at all on a single committed plunge (§2.2), which is the endgame's real gate. 
Loss: burned up — the astronaut caught outside above the heat-speed threshold; stranded — the run can no longer reach a landing (ship un-deorbitable, or astronaut unable to reach the ship); 0 HP (hard landing, or reentering too hot); or refused — the module delivered in scrap state, Armstrong's fury (finale only, §2.6). Every loss resets the current run: the ship respawns on the pad, cash and upgrades persist, and only this run's haul and its turnaround cost are lost — so failure stays a cheap, repeatable teaching beat and no save system enters scope. Full terminal-state partition and detectors: §2.7. 
Pillars: #Thematic — Scrapyard Spaceflight, blue-collar handmade spacecraft. #Mechanical — Physics Extraction, 2D flight and loot hauling. #Feeling — Anxiety from atmospheric reentry and the fear of wasting cargo value.
Audience & onboarding: the target player will play the full campaign — keyboard-literate, willing to learn a systems game across a session or two. The first run still teaches reentry through one cheap, legible failure — a post-mortem screen names the cause of death and the rule broken — but the game is tuned for the returning campaign pilot, not a single sitting. A full campaign is 12–15 successful runs at 4–8 wall-clock minutes each — short starter hops, longer expeditions near the top, the module's glint visible overhead from mid-campaign on — roughly 60–90 minutes of progression plus retries. Armstrong narrates over the radio — a dozen state-triggered one-liners ("Chute stays packed while she's glowing, kid") carrying both the tutorial hints and, later, the characterization the graded endings pay off.

2.2 Heat, Staging, Braking Passes & the Deploy Sequence
The heat bar is one gauge and one rule, and it runs in both directions: above roughly half orbital speed in atmosphere, heat builds and drag pulls; the deeper and faster, the hotter. It fills on the way up as well as on the way down. Heat dissipates over ~5 s once back below the safe speed. It’s capacity is a config value that the heat-shield upgrade raises (§2.5), so the bar the player reads is a fraction of a capacity that changes across the campaign; all figures below are in the simulator’s absolute bar units so they can be compared to a capacity directly. 
The climb. The ascent crosses the heat threshold at 583 m/s and spends 41.8 seconds above it, peaking at 47.5 bar units (measured, base ship) — a visible, alarming, entirely survivable fraction of the starter shield's capacity. That is deliberate: the player meets the heat mechanic on their very first flight and learns to read it somewhere it cannot kill them, and reentry stays the place it is dangerous. Peak heating on the climb is at 16,103 m and 794 m/s, not at maximum dynamic pressure, which is five times lower at 2,875 m (19.7 kPa) — heating goes as √density × speed³ while dynamic pressure goes as density × speed², so the two peaks sit 13 km apart. The effect belongs at the higher one. Air resistance is modelled on the way up as well as down, from the same density function: drag costs the climb 15.7 points of tank, a third of what the base ship comes home with, so sea-level density is a reentry-feel knob and a does-the-first-launch-work knob at the same time.
The ascent is exempt from the unstaged heat penalty. A rocket climbing prograde under thrust is in the aerodynamic configuration it was built for. The 3× unstaged multiplier below exists for a ship presenting a naked hull to the airflow on a braking pass, and it still applies there in full. This is a phase rule, not a smaller multiplier.
Staging and the shield. The heat shield sits behind the thruster and tank and is exposed only by staging, which jettisons them — one-way, no thrust after. Shape your descent while you still have an engine, then stage once the final arc is set. The shield itself is the health gauge: its sprite visibly ablates, pixels eroding from the exposed face as shield HP falls, so remaining protection is read off the ship.
The descent has two decisions, not one. How deep you commit — a shallower committed entry arrives cooler. And how many shallow braking passes (skims) you fly first — each one dips into the upper atmosphere, sheds speed, and comes back out. These two are substitutes, and that is the design problem the next rule solves: committing shallower buys the same reduction in entry speed a skim buys, for one heat cycle instead of two. Measured with the entry depth free to choose, the coolest single pass is as cool as or cooler than the coolest multi-pass descent at every scale height from 800 m to 3,100 m. No heat capacity separates them and no planet tuning does either.
The commit floor is the rule that makes braking passes worth flying. The player may not commit to an entry shallower than 8,000 m — fictionally, a minimum commit angle the salvaged RCS can hold, or a limit on how finely a scrap ship can aim an entry from orbit. Shallow-and-cool is off the table; skimming is the only way left to arrive slower. Hold the entry depth fixed and one skim is worth 0.42–0.53× on the peak (measured). Peak heat from the top of the band, with the floor in place (measured):
 load one committed plunge one skim, then commit
empty ship 144.7 86.2
full hold (≈1,398 kg) 203.7 125.5
Armstrong's module (3,600 kg) 268.8 196.5
The maxed heat shield's capacity is ~235 (to set). At that capacity the module cannot come home on a plunge — 268.8 against 235 — and comes home on one braking pass at 196.5, while an empty ship and a full hold still plunge freely. That is a 32% window between the full hold's plunge and the module's, bounded by a rule rather than by how finely anyone searched, which is why it is safe to build on. The hard constraint on the shop: no heat-shield tier may reach 268.8. A maxed shield that buys the plunge back deletes the win condition's mechanic. Do not try to force the full hold to skim as well — that window is (196.5, 203.7], 4% wide, a coincidence and not a mechanic.
Braking passes may be flown staged or unstaged, and it is a real choice. Staged: the shield is exposed, each pass costs 1× heat, and there is no thrust left to adjust periapsis between passes — you fly the rest of the descent on the trajectory you already have. Unstaged: the hull takes each pass at 3× heat, but the engine is still there and periapsis can be raised or lowered between passes. Committing early buys cool passes at the price of control; staying unstaged buys steering at triple heat.
The escalating thermal toll is the brake, and it is load-bearing. A braking pass costs 0 m/s of Δv — a skim at the right altitude drops apoapsis inside the atmosphere on its own — and about six minutes of flight (0.70 h direct against 0.80 h with a skim, measured). Nothing in the physics stops a player skimming indefinitely. What stops them is thermal fatigue: each heat cycle costs a percentage of shield plate, and that toll grows per cycle rather than staying flat. With a flat toll a 25-pass feathering descent costs about what a 4-pass one does; with growth above 1 it costs 847% of plate, which is to say it is not available. State it to the player as the cost of feathering, not as a tiebreaker. The benefit falls off from the other side too: the first pass does most of the work, because once apoapsis is inside the atmosphere there is little speed left to shed cheaply.
The deploy sequence. Plasma appears above a velocity threshold; a parachute deployed during plasma shreds. When the plasma clears — signaled by the salvaged CHUTE lamp flipping red to green, and telegraphed by hull glow and audio — deploy chute, then gear.
When the plate runs out. Once the shield is spent, cargo burns one slot per ~3 s while the bar is pinned at capacity, starting at the slot nearest the shield and propagating up the hold (pieces stow shield-end first, so early grabs ride closest to the fire); a burned piece becomes slag — value zero, mass retained, no protection for the slot above it (exception: Armstrong's module never slags — it degrades instead, §2.6, and the burn order skips its slot); hull damage begins only after all cargo.


2.3 Touchdown Quality
Landings are graded by vertical speed: soft under ~5 m/s; damage scales past that, capped per touchdown — a single landing, however hard, cannot destroy the ship from above a config'd hull fraction; no gear doubles it. Soft landings require deploying the chute early to shed enough speed. Chute descent speed grows with ship mass as the square root — confirmed by measurement, not assumed: doubling ship mass raises touchdown speed by 1.41×. The canopy is a stated design constant, not an inferred one: parachute_area_m2 and its drag coefficient live in the config (currently 630 m² at Cd 1.8, predicting 4.4 m/s at base storage with a full hold), so the simulator flies the physical canopy and the predicted descent speed is a claim the flight can contradict. Until the area was written down, the simulator solved it backwards out of the claimed descent speed and then measured that same speed back, and the parachute rule passed every audit it faced by construction. A run ends when the ship is staged, grounded, and crewed (§2.7): the haul auto-sells wherever you come down, minus the tow fee if you came down away from the yard (§2.5), and Armstrong's turnaround begins. Staging on the pad simply ends a mission that never left; an unstaged touchdown ends nothing and the ship can lift off again. 
2.4 The Hand Magnet & Towing
A salvaged electromagnet on a winch spool, clipped to the suit. Fire it at junk within ~1/8 screen; it clangs on and winds the piece to a short leash that trails behind you. One tether at a time. The piece adds its mass to yours: jetpack acceleration scales inversely with total mass. Release is instant and clean (power off, piece keeps its drift). Stow at the hold (E) to bank it. The greed decision: every trip out costs jetpack fuel and every stowed piece is permanent mass for the ride down. The magnet is one of the twelve purchases (§2.5); its tiers buy range and winch speed, and it is mass-neutral — it makes a crowded EVA window pay better without making the ride home harder. The suit carries a salvaged scale: a hold-mass readout, updated at each stow, its dial painted into three zones — green, amber, red: at this mass, a competent chute deploy lands soft, hard, or destructive. The two boundaries are config values calibrated from Sweep 1's logged touchdown speeds — a lookup, not a live prediction: no projected touchdown speed, no trajectory (the predictive sensor stays cut, §4.3), and like the lamps it assumes you fly the deploy sequence right. The greed decision is informed but never reversible in flight. The magnet also grips the ship: fire it at the hull from anywhere in tether range and winch yourself home — a dry jetpack within reach is an inconvenience, not §1's stranded loss. The suit carries one more salvaged instrument, the RETURN lamp: red when a bare suit — tethered piece dropped — at current fuel and drift could no longer reach tether range of the ship. Towing narrows the margin fast; release (RMB) is always the first remedy. Like the CHUTE lamp, it is a one-boolean caution light driven by a conservative closed-form estimate, not a trajectory predictor. Because the suit has no heat protection (§1's burn-up loss — fuel is no defense, and F is inert once heat builds), the lamp's load-bearing bounds are fuel and distance: red when the bare suit can no longer reach tether range of the ship. A time-to-interface clock is deferred to a stretch list, not built for the first pass — and note that crossing the interface is only fatal above the heat-speed threshold, so a slow descent to the ground is survivable and resolves as stranded (§2.7), not burn-up. 
2.5 Fuel, Economics & Upgrades
Every launch costs money: Armstrong rebuilds the jettisoned stage (thruster + tank), fills it, and repairs the hull/heat shield at a flat fee per %. Launch cost ≈ the value of 2–3 cheap pieces, so a lazy run still breaks even. Landing away from the junkyard costs a tow fee: free inside a short radius of the pad (~1,200 m), rising linearly with distance beyond it, and clamped at 50% of the haul's value — a bad landing site is a bill, never a wiped run, and never a reason not to come down. Mercy floor: if cash can't cover the turnaround, Armstrong provides it anyway and cash simply stops at zero — a grounded, intact ship can always launch, so the economy can never strand the player. The auto-sell, the tow and the mercy-floor turnaround aren't generosity: Armstrong runs a drone recovery net and fronts each launch against your next haul. The drones can lift, not land: orbital tugs that shove mass around in vacuum or haul it upward — collecting grounded hauls and re-parking the module (§2.6) are both ascents — but nothing they carry survives reentry, and none of it is ever simulated: the net is fiction, zero build cost. The only thing in this world that brings mass down intact is a piloted ship behind a staged shield. That's why he needs you. You're not being helped, you're in his debt — and the first three turnarounds are free outright ("Teaching's part of the debt, kid"), so the campaign's budgeted learning failures cost their haul but never their launch. Upgrades - six parts ×  two tiers = 12 purchases: Fuel tank (capacity), Thruster (Thrust), Storage (cargo slots), Heat shield (heat-bar capacity and lighter), Parachute (canopy area), Hand magnet (range and winch speed). Every part ships at a starter value and can be bought up twice; nothing ships at a fixed mid-tier anymore.


The heat-shield track is the one with reach, and it runs thick-to-thin: the starter ship carries a heavy scrap-steel slab, and each tier — ceramic tile, then aerospace composite — is both lighter and stronger. Stronger means a larger heat-bar capacity, not more plate: the bar's capacity is the parameter that decides whether a given load has to aerobrake (§2.2), so putting it on the shield makes the endgame's difficulty something the player shops for. Ceiling on the whole track: every tier stays below 268.8 bar units, the module's plunge peak. It is also the only purchase that reduces ship mass — the one systemic counterweight to storage-driven landing difficulty.


Storage is double-edged: a bigger hold raises the profit ceiling and the difficulty of the flight home. The shop labels every purchase's mass effect — storage as mass-adding, the heat shield as the only mass-reducing buy — so the storage-first difficulty spike is legible rather than a mystery the player blames on their own flying.
The fuel tank's first tier has a specific job, and it is the clearest progression beat in the campaign. The base ship reaches the floor of the band on a ballistic arc with 29.5% of its tank left and a 97-second EVA window (measured). It can technically circularise at the floor, but arrives with 0% left and nothing to deorbit on — so the arc is the first launch's route, and the first tank upgrade (10.9% left after circularising) is what makes orbit a real option. Say it in the shop label: this is the buy that turns a hop into a stay.
Pacing targets: world scale is decreed, not discovered — but it is now decreed and written down (crew/planet.lock.json), not re-derived per session. Prices are generated by rule rather than listed: with 12–15 runs and 12 purchases, each upgrade costs roughly the net profit of one decent run at its tier, and the altitude value gradient supplies the escalation. Three to five learning failures are budgeted into the campaign math, funded by the free turnarounds; if they still stretch the campaign, length flexes to 15–18 runs before any price moves.


2.6 Junk Value & Altitude
One continuous orbital band, 50,000 m to 280,000 m, with a value gradient — no tiers, no discrete altitudes to keep in sync. Cheap scrap panels low, denser and more valuable wreckage the higher you go: a piece's value multiplier interpolates on its altitude from 1.0 at the floor to 5.5 at the ceiling. The player picks their own working altitude inside the envelope and picks their route to it: a ballistic arc never pays for circularisation, which is most of the delta-v, but it comes back in faster and hotter and gives a short working window; circularising costs the rest of the tank and buys time. Both are legal at every altitude, and the bargain is different at each. Value scales with mass on top of the altitude gradient, so the good stuff fights you on the tether and on the way down — height is where the money is, and the reentry risk rises with it on its own. That is the difficulty curve, produced by one gradient instead of three bands.
At the very top hangs Armstrong's module: Apollo-era hardware that predates — and outlived — the cascade. It is a single slot but ~3,600 kg, roughly doubling the ship's mass — it costs a maxed hold's mass budget, not its slot count — and at that mass it cannot be brought down on a committed plunge at any legal entry depth (§2.2). The finale is an aerobraking flight, by construction. It is visible long before it is reachable: from mid-band up, its glint renders as a distinct off-palette glimmer overhead, and the first time the player crosses half-envelope Armstrong marks it ("See her glinting up there? That's the one. Not yet, kid.") — the mid-campaign runs climb toward something the player has seen, and the finale is planted well before its delivery terms arrive. The module cannot be destroyed, only degraded — pristine → damaged → scrap — dropping one state per sustained heat exposure and one per hard touchdown. Its condition at touchdown selects the outcome: pristine wins Armstrong's triumph; damaged wins his disappointment, at reduced payout; scrap is refused — Armstrong's fury, a loss. The module is conserved: on any run end where it is undelivered — released on an arc, towed into a stranded loss, aboard a destroyed ship, burned over while tethered, or refused — the drones return it to orbit reset to damaged. The scar persists: pristine delivery is a first-attempt-only triumph, while a winnable damaged delivery always remains reachable. Its condition is read off the module itself — the sprite visibly scorches per state, reusing the shield's ablation pattern — and Armstrong barks at each state change, so the grade is never a surprise held for touchdown. Destruction is still never a path to victory: 0 HP is a loss regardless of what's aboard, and the custody rule hands the module back at damaged — scrap arises only from two in-run degradations, and scrap never wins. A hard touchdown — vertical speed above the module's own degradation threshold, a config value independent of the ship's damage line — degrades it exactly one state, so a pristine module cannot be lost to a single bad flare; losing the finale takes two mistakes (heat degradation plus a hard landing), and the ship's destruction loses the run but returns the module at damaged. Armstrong states these delivery terms over the radio the first time the module is tethered, so the grade is communicated before it can be committed; the hold-mass scale (§2.4) is the compensating information in flight. Sweep 1's final question is answered from data it already produces: every cell logs touchdown speed, so if pristine fails at the ship's damage line, the module's threshold is set just above the competent-pilot floor read off the existing table — the lever is chosen after the sweep, no rerun night required.


2.7 Terminal States & Loss
Every reachable end state resolves to exactly one outcome, each with a detector — no state escapes the partition. Landed (ship grounded, staged, and crewed): run ends, haul auto-sells net of any tow fee; a module aboard in pristine or damaged state commits its ending — success. An uncrewed ship grounding is a non-event — the ship parks inert and the run continues until the astronaut resolves it: board the parked ship → Landed; touch terrain not aboard → stranded. Refused: a module delivered in scrap state — loss; the run resets and the drones return the module to orbit for a re-fly (§2.6). 0 HP: ship destroyed on a hard landing, or by reentering too hot — the bar pinned at capacity long enough to burn through plate, then cargo, then hull (§2.2); a player feathering an endless string of braking passes reaches the same end by way of the escalating toll — loss (module run: indestructibility preserves the module; the custody rule returns it to orbit at damaged, §2.6). Burned up: astronaut on EVA in atmosphere above the heat-speed threshold — loss. Stranded: a single unwinnability check fires when the run can no longer reach a landing and no recovery input remains — (a) ship undeorbitable (out of fuel, or staged so the tank is gone) on an arc whose periapsis never enters the atmosphere; (b) astronaut jetpack-dry beyond tether reach of the ship; (c) astronaut touching terrain while not aboard the ship; (d) ship staged, chute shredded, on a descent whose minimum achievable touchdown speed exceeds the destruction cap — a conservative closed-form check, like the lamps — resolved immediately as 0 HP rather than flown out in real time. Warp is disabled the instant this check arms, so a confused player cannot fast-forward an unwinnable state — loss. Note that a staged ship on a braking pass is not stranded: an aerobraking orbit has its periapsis inside the atmosphere by definition, so it will come down. On any loss the run resets: ship respawns on the pad, cash and upgrades persist, only this run's haul and turnaround are lost. The shop loop already persists progression, so no save/checkpoint subsystem is in scope. 
3. AI Architecture (Coder → QA → Playtester)
Junkstronaut's difficulty emerges from a single closed feedback loop: cargo mass raises reentry heat, heat forces braking passes and delays safe parachute deployment, later deployment increases touchdown speed, touchdown speed destroys cargo, and cargo value funds the upgrades that raise carrying capacity and heat-bar capacity together. The loop is coupled and nonlinear, so its behavior is determined empirically by sampling the parameter space. The three agents implement that measurement.
Coder: Implements one mechanic per session as GDScript. Every tunable lives in a single config/game_params.tres; hardcoded literals are a rejection condition, so any later session can understand a number from one small file instead of from the codebase. Two constants that were previously implicit are now required entries, because both produced audits that checked a claim against itself: reentry.commit_floor_m (§2.2) and landing.parachute_area_m2 with its drag coefficient (§2.3).
QA: Writes a GUT regression suite from the spec and holds the ticket closed until it passes. Roughly a dozen invariants — among them: staging is one-way and kills all thrust; the chute shreds during plasma; shield HP depletes before cargo, cargo before hull, and burn order is shield-proximity order; slag retains mass with zero value and no protection; each shield tier is strictly lighter and strictly higher-capacity than the last, and no tier reaches the module's plunge peak; the committed entry cannot be aimed shallower than commit_floor_m; the per-cycle thermal toll strictly grows, so feathering is never free; an unstaged braking pass pays exactly 3× and a prograde ascent pays 1×; towed mass scales acceleration inversely; no-gear landings double damage; no single touchdown destroys the ship from above the cap's hull fraction; the tow fee never exceeds 50% of haul value and cash never blocks a launch; a piece's value multiplier is strictly increasing in its altitude; staged + grounded + crewed always ends the run and returns to the shop, and an uncrewed grounding ends nothing; a scrap-state module delivery resolves to refused — a loss, never a win; the module never exits play — every terminal state with it undelivered returns it to orbit at damaged; a scripted tow of a ship-mass object from spawn range completes within jetpack fuel at starter values (validates the win condition's EVA leg — the one act the sweep cannot fly); and every reachable terminal state resolves to exactly one §2.7 outcome, with no state (staged-in-orbit, mid-aerobrake, slow-falling astronaut, grounded astronaut, uncrewed ship grounding mid-bailout, post-shred doomed descent) escaping the partition — Landed requires the astronaut aboard, so no run ends while the pilot is in the air.  Determinism gets its own pair: two headless runs with identical config and scripted inputs are bit-identical, and a run flown at warp is bit-identical to the same inputs at 1×. The warp locks (§2.1), tether-hull recovery, astronaut burn-up, and RETURN lamp bounds (§2.4) are enforced verbatim as tests rather than restated here. A red test returns to the Coder with the failing assertion as the bug report. 
Playtester: Runs headless Godot across a grid of config values and reports what the player would experience. The harness free-runs physics faster than realtime, decoupled from the 60 Hz render clock; a ~200-cell grid at ~5 sim-minutes per run costs wall-clock hours that are measured, not assumed: the Day-1 determinism test already runs the integrator headless, and logging its step rate prices the sweep immediately (18,000 fixed steps per cell × 200 cells against measured steps/sec). If a full sweep projects past one overnight run, the grid coarsens — wider steps, same axes — until it fits; sweep scope flexes, sweep existence never does. Sweeps run overnight, so a rerun costs calendar nights, not focused daytime hours. Each cell carries a per-run timeout that classifies a non-terminating run as a distinct outcome rather than hanging the grid. Flights are flown by an autopilot: a phase state machine — ascend, coast, load, deorbit, brake N times, stage, wait-for-green, chute, gear — with two real controllers, ascent guidance (burn and pitch to a target apoapsis) and a descent planner (choose entry depth subject to the commit floor, and a pass count). EVA is not flown; at the working altitude the harness injects N slots of cargo mass directly into the hold, so the sweep measures physics and mass, not tether dexterity. The load set must include the module at ~3,600 kg: an earlier version ran empty / half / full hold, which meant the heaviest object in the game — the win condition — had never been flown at all. The pilot flies by the player's own instruments — stage once the final arc is set or the tank runs dry, chute at first CHUTE-green, gear below deploy altitude — so every sweep doubles as a check that informed use of those instruments is enough to win. Its schedule derives from config/game_params.tres (a hardcoded trajectory is a rejection condition, like any literal), and a failed run logs which phase died and why — never reached apoapsis, burned through the bar on pass 2, chute window never opened, touchdown over threshold — so a red grid cell is a diagnosis, not a shrug. The autopilot flies only the happy path: it validates beatable; the QA invariants validate un-soft-lockable. The sweep answers nine questions, in campaign order: does the base ship's climb survive its own launch —a config whose first flight burns up must fail, not pass reachability without comment? Does a starter arc leave time above the band floor to tether and stow at least one piece before the RETURN lamp forces the trip home? Does a lazy run break even after the tow fee? Does a tier-1 ship at a modest altitude graze plasma, yet land under the damage threshold even with a late chute? Can a full-hold ship reach the top of the band, reenter, and land under 5 m/s? Is the module's descent infeasible as a plunge and feasible with one braking pass, at every shield tier — the endgame's whole mechanic, judged on survivable peak heat and not on cost? Does a competent playthrough reach the module in 12–15 runs at 4–8 minutes each — neither a demo nor a grind — and does a rough one, with four early failures, still finish inside 18? Is the module run beatable at full upgrades? And what module threshold does the logged touchdown-speed table support — is pristine achievable at the ship's damage line, or does the module's independent threshold carry it? Each report proposes one candidate value set, including the scale's two band boundaries read off the touchdown-speed table. The harness is built once and run twice — the reentry sweep and the economy sweep. The shield adds one axis; only its endpoints (starter slab, aerospace composite) are swept, bounding the envelope. 
4. Technical Strategy
4.1 Stack
Godot 4.x (2D), GDScript. Custom fixed-timestep gravity integration on CharacterBody2D (deterministic). GUT for tests, run headless. One config resource for all tunables. Claude Code subscription; Git. Art: pixel packs + Deep-Fold planet generator; Aseprite for gap sprites only. The world is a fixed input, not a search space: radius, gravity, atmosphere top, scale height and sea-level density are locked in crew/planet.lock.json and no agent may re-derive them — an earlier arrangement let a research agent invent a planet per run, which moved 73% of a grid's scores between rounds and made every measured number a property of a world the next run might not produce. Positioning: a short-session descendant of the Learn to Fly / Into Space launch-and-upgrade loop crossed with Hardspace: Shipbreaker's salvage and Lunar Lander's reentry tension — its wrinkle is that cargo value and survivability are the same variable. Ships as an itch.io desktop build at end of week. 
4.2 Token Budget
Claude Pro, seven days, Sonnet as the default model with Opus reserved for the two hardest problems (the fixed-timestep integrator and the heat/drag coupling). Verify, against Anthropic's documentation before Day 1: current Pro-tier Opus access, daily-usage limits, and — the load-bearing check — that base Pro throughput sustains the ~1.3M-token week at all — if Opus isn't available at the working tier, both hard problems fall back to Sonnet with extra QA passes.
Planned cadence: Coder sessions target 40–60k tokens — one mechanic, its config entries, and the two or three files it touches. QA sessions run ~25k, since the test suite reads the spec and the invariant list rather than the implementation. Playtester sessions run ~30k, because the sweep executes outside the model context and only the outcome table returns. The autopilot is a scheduled Coder session of its own (40–60k), landing mid-week once the integrator, flight, and reentry exist — immediately before Sweep 1 consumes it; its descent planner is part of that session, not an addition to it. That is roughly 150–200k tokens per day, ~1.2M across the week. The cadence assumes rework: roughly one in three Coder sessions bounces off a red QA test once, at ~15–20k per return trip, and UI/content is a budget line, not a remainder: HUD, shop labels, post-mortem screen, the two lamps, the mass scale, the ablating-shield readout, and landing grades are three Coder sessions (~40k each), and the narrative layer — the dozen barks and the three ending screens — is a fourth. All four are scheduled days 5–6, ahead of feel-tuning. That raises planned spend by ~120k, shrinking contingency to ~150k — which moves the cut-order trigger earlier, it does not remove it. ~20% of the weekly budget (~250k) is contingency, spent only on bounces — if it runs dry, the §4.4 cut order activates top-down. Human budget: 14–16 Coder sessions plus QA and Playtester sessions over seven days is 2–3 review-and-integrate cycles per day, on top of the Day-3 towing gate, art integration, sweep-table reading, and the feel-tuning reserved for me — roughly 4–6 focused hours per day. The token budget closes; the human is the bottleneck — so the cut order carries a second, human-denominated trigger: if review-and-integrate cycles fall below two per day for two consecutive days, it fires regardless of token health.


4.3 Cut From the Scope (and why)
* Multi-tether towing & shear. One tether, mass-only physics — the trips-vs-greed decision survives in coarser form.
* Size classes, fragile flag, compactor, crane magnet, oversized junk. All exist to serve the endgame. Every piece is one slot; the module is simply very heavy.
* Discrete altitude bands. One continuous envelope with a value gradient does the same job with nothing to keep in sync (§2.6). 
* Art Director agent; multi-tether tech demo; predictive reentry sensor (the CHUTE lamp is a one-boolean caution light, not this);  controller support
4.4 Risks
*  The autopilot. The sweep's real cost is the pilot, not the harness (budgeted in §4.2; chain integrator → flight → reentry → pilot). It now owns one more controller than it did — a descent planner that picks entry depth against the commit floor and a pass count — because a pilot that only flies plunges cannot answer the endgame question at all. If it misses its timebox, the fallback is physics-only descent sweeps — spawn the ship at altitude, mass, and velocity on a scripted stage/brake/chute schedule and integrate down. Reentry questions stay measurable, but this drops the economy and pacing questions, a reduced deliverable stated plainly to the grader. Because the sweeps are the assignment, the autopilot is never-cut: pulled one session earlier, first claim on the §4.2 contingency. 
* The commit floor has to be legible in the cockpit. It is the rule the whole endgame rests on, and it is a rule about a number the player cannot see directly. If the entry-aim UI does not make "you may not commit shallower than this" obvious and physical, the mechanic reads as the game refusing an input. Fallback: express it as a hard stop on the aim indicator with the reason on the HUD, and let Armstrong say it once.
* The starter shield's capacity is unchosen, and it sets the tone of the first flight. The climb peaks at 47.5 bar units. Against the old fixed capacity of 100 that was half the bar — a good, frightening, safe first lesson. Against a capacity ladder topping out near 235 the starter tier's value is a design choice nobody has made, and it decides whether the first launch looks alarming or looks like nothing at all. Pick it for the lesson, then check it against the empty ship's 144.7 plunge.
* Reentry tuning. Sweep 1 exists to compress this: the grid answers survivability, I only tune feel. The cost question is settled and should not be reopened — one pass is the cheapest descent under every ablation keying measured, at every altitude and load, so the design's requirement is feasibility (the module cannot plunge) and never cost. Fallback: pre-agreed constants — fixed heat rate per unit overspeed, fixed plasma threshold — tuned only by moving the safe-speed line and the commit floor.
* * Towing feels sluggish rather than weighty. Day-3 gate decides; fallback is rigid attachment (piece snaps to the suit, contributes mass only).
* * The integrator is a universal dependency. Every mechanic and the Playtester's determinism assumption sit on it. It is built first, and the determinism invariant (identical config + scripted inputs → bit-identical trajectories) must pass on Day 1 before any other Coder session opens. Fallback: if Godot's physics server is not bit-stable on ground contact, touchdown moves to custom collision on the fixed-timestep integrator — the determinism invariant then depends on no engine-managed contact at all.
* * The cut order. The cut order. If the week runs hot, features die in this order, nothing below the line touched until everything above it is gone: (1) warp — world scale shrinks to compensate; an early-week cut only, off the table once Sweep 1 fires, and note that braking passes lean on warp for their coast time; (2) the reduced Sweep 2 grid — pre-declared coarser steps on the same axes, fired by a lost overnight window, always forfeited before the days 5–6 UI block; (3) the module's exception bundle — skip-slot burn, state ladder, and re-fly custody collapse to a binary intact/scrap indestructible piece, fired by token/rework overrun; (4) the shield's pixel-ablation readout — falls back to a plain HP bar; (5) the CHUTE/RETURN lamp polish — falls back to bare boolean lights. Never cut: the integrator, staged reentry, the commit floor, the tether, the shop loop, the mercy floor, the autopilot and both sweeps — the methodology is the assignment.
4.5 Open, and deliberately not written down as settled
4.5 Open, and deliberately not written down as settled
Is 97 seconds a workable EVA window? It is the base ship's whole first launch, and whether that is enough time to fly out, tether a piece and stow it is a judgement nobody has made in play. If it is too tight, the fix is a bigger arc, not a bigger tank: apex barely moves across the entire shop (56.2 → 57.0 km) because the climb is thrust-limited, not fuel-limited.
How a braking pass is distinguished from a long shallow entry. Currently by pass count, which is brittle — whether a ship exits the air is an accident of where periapsis sits. Arc swept inside the atmosphere measures the intent directly: an evading shallow dive sweeps 118°, a genuine skim-then-commit sweeps 338°, and a threshold near 270° would separate them physically. Not implemented, and the pass-count rule is adequate as long as the commit floor holds.
Whether the ascent heat rule survives a different world. The exempt climb reads 47.5 on the locked planet and 157 on a synthetic test world, so "the launch survives" is a property of this planet and not a guarantee of the rule. The audit reports the climb's peak heat; making it fail a world whose first flight burns up is small, separate work.




5. Revision and Growth
* nowRevision 3 — written against measurements, 2026-07-28. Everything here came out of flying the design in a simulator rather than reasoning about it, and three of the six were corrections of things this document previously asserted. 
* The commit floor (§2.2). The new mechanic, and the one the endgame rests on. Skimming and the committed plunge are substitutes: committing shallower buys the same reduction in entry speed a skim buys, for one heat cycle instead of two, and with entry depth free no heat capacity and no planet separates a plunge from a skimmed descent. A floor on how shallow you may commit restores the mechanic — with entry held fixed, one pass is worth 0.42–0.53× on the peak. 
* Multi-pass is a feasibility rule, not a cost rule. The earlier goal — "the cheapest descent from the top should be 2 passes" — is unsatisfiable and is withdrawn. One pass is the cheapest under every ablation keying measured. What the design actually wanted is that the heaviest haul cannot plunge at all, which is both easier to state and closer to what the mechanic is for. 
* One band with a continuous value gradient (§2.6). Replaces three discrete bands. Value interpolates 1.0 → 5.5 on altitude across 50,000–280,000 m, so the risk curve and the value curve rise together with nothing to keep in sync. 
* Twelve purchases across six parts (§2.5), replacing nine across three, and the heat-shield tiers buy heat-bar capacity. That is not a shop-size change: bar capacity is the parameter that decides whether a load must aerobrake, so making it a purchase puts the endgame's difficulty in the player's hands — bounded by the rule that no tier may reach the module's plunge peak.
* The heat bar runs both ways (§2.2). The climb was accumulating heat all along and nothing ever read it; on the shipped planet an unexempted ascent peaked at 1.4× the old capacity, meaning the base ship burned up before reaching the junk and every reachability check passed it. The ascent is exempt from the 3× unstaged penalty as a phase rule, and the climb becomes the player's first, safe lesson in reading the gauge.
* The world is locked, and two constants that were being inferred are now stated. crew/planet.lock.json fixes the planet; commit_floor_m and parachute_area_m2 are required config. Both of the latter existed as claims the simulator solved backwards out of their own consequences, so both passed every audit they ever faced by construction.
* Revision 2 — the agent-review pass that produced the Final draft.
* Heat shield becomes an upgrade track. Agent reviewed finding. The shield is the only mass-reducing buy — the deliberate counterweight to storage-driven landing difficulty. Combined with shield-as-HP (visible ablation) and the slag/burn-order model, this reworks the core reentry tension from a flat heat bar into a resource you spend and upgrade. Revision 3 extends it: the tiers now buy the bar's capacity as well.
* The prize + graded endings. Agent reviewed finding. Satellite → Apollo-era module that degrades (pristine → damaged → scrap) instead of being destroyed, and its condition selects the ending and scales payout. This converts a binary win into a graded finale with narrative stakes — and it's what Armstrong's radio barks pay off.
* §2.7 Terminal States + mercy floor. Agent reviewed finding. A formal partition of every end state with a detector each, plus the guarantee that the economy can never strand you. This makes the game provably soft-lock-proof — a design-robustness change that also becomes a QA invariant.
* The autopilot reframing (§3 + §4.4). Agent reviewed finding. The sweep has a real pilot — a phase state machine that flies by the player's own instruments, with cargo injected instead of EVA flown. §4.4 promotes the autopilot to the top risk and makes it never-cut, because the sweeps are the graded methodology.
* Warp (hold T). My own call, prompted by human review. Keeps tall coasts cheap, but it is entangled with world scale and Sweep 1 — and Revision 3 gave it a second job, since a multi-pass descent coasts between braking passes.
